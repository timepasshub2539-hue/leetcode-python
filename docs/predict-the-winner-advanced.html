Predict the Winner — 🔴 Advanced (3/3) — LeetCode Daily Python Solution (stop tracking two scores)

General

Introduction

Every so often a LeetCode problem looks like it needs more state than it actually does, and Predict the Winner is the textbook example. On the surface it feels like you need to track two running scores, one for each player, across every possible sequence of moves. That instinct is exactly what interviewers are testing — not whether you can write a recursive function, but whether you can notice that the two-score version of the problem is doing twice the work it needs to.

This problem shows up constantly in interview loops because it's a clean entry point into an entire family of questions: "two players take turns, both play optimally, who wins?" Once you've internalized the trick here, you can walk into Stone Game II, Stone Game III, or any variant of alternating-turn optimal play and immediately recognize the shape of the solution. The core idea — reducing two competing scores down to a single relative value — is one of the most reusable tricks in dynamic programming interviews.

Problem Overview

You're given an array of numbers laid out in a row. Two players take turns picking a number from either end of the array — the leftmost remaining number or the rightmost remaining number — and whatever they pick gets added to their own personal total. Player 1 always moves first. Both players are trying to end up with the highest score possible, and both are assumed to play perfectly, meaning neither one ever makes a mistake or a shortsighted move.

Your job is to determine whether Player 1 can guarantee a final score that is greater than or equal to Player 2's score, assuming both players play as well as they possibly can. Note the "greater than or equal to" — a tie counts as a win for Player 1. That detail is easy to skim past and easy to get wrong in an interview, so it's worth committing to memory early.

Example

Take nums = [1, 5, 2].

If Player 1 grabs the 2 from the right end thinking it's the biggest single number available, the array left is [1, 5]. Now it's Player 2's turn, and Player 2 simply takes the 5, leaving Player 1 with only the 1. Final scores: Player 1 has 2 + 1 = 3, Player 2 has 5. Player 1 loses, badly.

If Player 1 had instead taken the 1 from the left, the remaining array is [5, 2], and Player 2 would take the 5, leaving Player 1 the 2. Final scores: Player 1 has 1 + 2 = 3, Player 2 has 5. Same losing outcome.

No matter which end Player 1 picks first, Player 2 can always respond in a way that leaves Player 1 behind. So for nums = [1, 5, 2], the correct answer is false. This one array is worth memorizing, because it's the standard counterexample used to show that greedy strategies don't work here.

Intuition

The first instinct most people have is to simulate the game directly: write a recursive function that tries "Player 1 takes left" and "Player 1 takes right," have Player 2 do the same thing on the remaining subarray, and compare final scores at the end. That works, but it means carrying two numbers — Player 1's running total and Player 2's running total — through every branch of the recursion. That's more state than the problem actually needs.

Here's the observation that unlocks the efficient solution: this game is zero-sum. Every number in the array eventually belongs to exactly one player, so the two scores are always tied together — whatever isn't in Player 1's pile is in Player 2's. That means you don't need to track both totals separately. You only need to track the difference between whoever is moving next and everyone who moves after them.

Define diff(i, j) as the maximum score advantage that the player whose turn it currently is can guarantee, when only the subarray from index i to index j is left to pick from. "Advantage" here means: my score minus my opponent's score, from this point forward, assuming both of us play perfectly for the rest of the game.

Why does this collapse work? Because after the current player picks a number, the roles flip — the opponent becomes the "current player" on a smaller subarray. Whatever advantage the opponent can guarantee for themselves is exactly a disadvantage for me. So instead of two separate maximization problems, you get one recurrence where the opponent's optimal play shows up as a negative sign. That's the entire insight. Everything else is just filling in a table correctly.

Once you frame the problem this way, the original question — "does Player 1 end up with a score at least as high as Player 2's?" — becomes simply: "is the score gap for the whole array, from Player 1's perspective, greater than or equal to zero?"

Brute Force Solution

Idea: Directly recurse on diff(i, j) without storing any previously computed answers. At each subarray, the current player chooses whichever end gives them the better outcome after subtracting the opponent's best response on the remainder.

Algorithm: 1. Base case: if i == j, only one number is left, so the current player must take it. Return nums[i]. 2. Recursive case: return max(nums[i] - diff(i + 1, j), nums[j] - diff(i, j - 1)). 3. The overall answer is diff(0, n - 1) >= 0.

Advantages: Trivial to write, and it's a direct, obviously-correct translation of the problem statement. Great as a first pass to establish correctness before optimizing, and it demonstrates you understand the recursive structure.

Disadvantages: The same (i, j) subrange gets recomputed an enormous number of times. diff(1, 2), for example, can be reached by first taking from the left of diff(0, 2) or from the right of diff(1, 3) — multiple different paths land on the exact same subproblem, and without caching, each path re-derives it from scratch.

Time complexity: O(2ⁿ), because every state branches into two recursive calls and nothing is cached. Space complexity: O(n), for the recursion call stack depth.

Optimal Solution

The recursion tree above is not actually a tree — it's a directed acyclic graph, because the same (i, j) pair is reachable from multiple parents. There are only O(n²) distinct (i, j) pairs total (since i and j both range over n positions), so if you cache each one the first time you compute it, you never redo the same work twice.

The cleanest way to fill this table is bottom-up, ordered by subarray length. Here's why that ordering works: dp[i][j] only ever depends on dp[i+1][j] and dp[i][j-1] — both are strictly shorter subarrays. If you process subarrays in order of increasing length, every value a given cell depends on has already been computed by the time you need it. This is just a topological sort of the dependency graph, and it means you never need recursion or a "visited" set.

Length What's being filled
1 dp[i][i] = nums[i] for every i (base case)
2 dp[i][i+1] = max(nums[i] - nums[i+1], nums[i+1] - nums[i])
3+ dp[i][j] = max(nums[i] - dp[i+1][j], nums[j] - dp[i][j-1])

The final answer lives in dp[0][n-1]: if it's greater than or equal to zero, Player 1 can force a win or a tie.

Python Code

from typing import List


def predict_the_winner(nums: List[int]) -> bool:
    n = len(nums)
    if n <= 1:
        return True  # Player 1 takes the only pile, or there's nothing to lose with

    # dp[i][j] = best score advantage the current mover can guarantee
    # for the subarray nums[i..j]
    dp = [[0] * n for _ in range(n)]

    # Base case: single-element subarrays
    for i in range(n):
        dp[i][i] = nums[i]

    # Fill by increasing subarray length so dependencies are always ready
    for length in range(2, n + 1):
        for i in range(n - length + 1):
            j = i + length - 1
            take_left = nums[i] - dp[i + 1][j]
            take_right = nums[j] - dp[i][j - 1]
            dp[i][j] = max(take_left, take_right)

    return dp[0][n - 1] >= 0

Code Walkthrough

  • n <= 1 handles the trivial case where there's at most one pile — Player 1 either has nothing to lose or simply takes the single number, so the answer is always True.
  • dp is an n x n table where dp[i][j] will eventually hold the score advantage the current player can force on the subarray nums[i..j].
  • The base-case loop fills in every dp[i][i]: with one number left, the player to move just takes it, so their guaranteed advantage is that number's value.
  • The outer loop walks through increasing subarray lengths, starting at 2, because length-1 subarrays are already handled.
  • The inner loop slides the window: for a given length, i ranges over every valid starting index, and j is derived from i and length.
  • take_left represents picking nums[i]: you gain nums[i], then your opponent becomes the "current player" on nums[i+1..j] and plays to maximize their own advantage — which is a loss of dp[i+1][j] from your perspective, hence the subtraction.
  • take_right is the mirror image for picking nums[j].
  • dp[i][j] is whichever of the two choices leaves the current player better off.
  • The final return checks whether Player 1's guaranteed advantage over the full array is non-negative — this is where the tie-goes-to-Player-1 rule gets encoded.

Dry Run

Let's trace nums = [1, 5, 233, 7], so n = 4.

Length 1 (base cases): dp[0][0]=1, dp[1][1]=5, dp[2][2]=233, dp[3][3]=7

Length 2: - dp[0][1] = max(1 - dp[1][1], 5 - dp[0][0]) = max(1 - 5, 5 - 1) = max(-4, 4) = 4 - dp[1][2] = max(5 - dp[2][2], 233 - dp[1][1]) = max(5 - 233, 233 - 5) = max(-228, 228) = 228 - dp[2][3] = max(233 - dp[3][3], 7 - dp[2][2]) = max(233 - 7, 7 - 233) = max(226, -226) = 226

Length 3: - dp[0][2] = max(nums[0] - dp[1][2], nums[2] - dp[0][1]) = max(1 - 228, 233 - 4) = max(-227, 229) = 229 - dp[1][3] = max(nums[1] - dp[2][3], nums[3] - dp[1][2]) = max(5 - 226, 7 - 228) = max(-221, -221) = -221

Length 4: - dp[0][3] = max(nums[0] - dp[1][3], nums[3] - dp[0][2]) = max(1 - (-221), 7 - 229) = max(222, -222) = 222

dp[0][3] = 222, which is >= 0, so predict_the_winner([1, 5, 233, 7]) returns True. This matches the actual optimal-play result of 234 to 12 — Player 1 secures the 233 by correctly reasoning several moves ahead, even though it isn't available on the very first pick.

Complexity Analysis

Time: O(n²). There are O(n²) distinct (i, j) pairs, and each one is computed in O(1) time from two already-known smaller subproblems, so the total work is proportional to the number of cells in the table.

Space: O(n²) for the full DP table. This is correct and tight for the straightforward version — no cell is ever visited more than once, and every cell is genuinely needed at least once to compute a later one.

Alternative Solutions

Top-down memoization is a direct translation of the brute-force recursion, adding a cache (dictionary or 2D array) keyed on (i, j). It's often the fastest to write correctly under interview pressure because it mirrors the recurrence exactly. The tradeoff: Python's recursion depth can become a real issue, since the recursion depth scales with n, and for large inputs you risk hitting RecursionError — this is the practical reason to prefer the bottom-up version, not just style preference.

Space-optimized rolling array (O(n) space): Since dp[i][j] only reads from dp[i+1][j] (the row below) and dp[i][j-1] (the same row, one column to the left), you can collapse the 2D table into a single 1D array — but the iteration direction matters. You must iterate i downward within each length so that dp[j-1] still holds the current length's freshly written value rather than a stale one from the previous length. This is a good one to have ready if an interviewer asks you to optimize space on the spot.

Edge Cases

  • Empty array: Not a valid input per the constraints, but defensively, an empty array trivially favors Player 1 (nothing to lose).
  • Single element: dp[i][i] = nums[i] is the base case — Player 1 takes the only pile and wins by definition.
  • Two equal elements, e.g. nums = [2, 2]: dp[0][1] = max(2 - 2, 2 - 2) = 0. Since 0 >= 0, the answer is True — this is the exact case interviewers use to verify you implemented "tie counts as a win" instead of "strictly greater."
  • All identical values: The score gap collapses to zero regardless of strategy, and the tie rule always resolves this to True.
  • Large arrays with extreme value swings (like [1, 5, 233, 7] above): watch for correctly matching i/j subtraction terms — swapping them silently produces a plausible-looking but wrong number.

Common Mistakes

  1. Using > 0 instead of >= 0 in the final check. This flips the answer on every tie case and is the single most common bug in this problem.
  2. Tracking two separate scores through the recursion instead of the single relative difference, which doubles the state and complicates the code for no benefit.
  3. Forgetting to memoize the top-down version, leading to exponential blowup that passes small test cases but times out on larger ones.
  4. Filling the bottom-up table in the wrong order — iterating by i and j directly instead of by increasing subarray length, which reads uninitialized cells.
  5. Reasoning greedily — assuming the player should always take the locally larger end value, which the [1, 5, 2] example directly disproves.
  6. Getting the subtraction backwards in the recurrence (writing dp[i+1][j] - nums[i] instead of nums[i] - dp[i+1][j]), which silently inverts the whole result.
  7. Off-by-one errors in the length loop bounds, particularly forgetting that i must stop early enough that j = i + length - 1 stays within bounds.

Interview Questions

  • Why does subtracting the opponent's optimal result work instead of writing a separate minimization branch?
  • How would you modify this if a player could take up to k elements from either end per turn?
  • What changes if the win condition requires strictly outscoring the opponent rather than tying?
  • Can you prove that with an even-length array, Player 1 can always force at least a tie?
  • How would you reduce this solution's space complexity from O(n²) to O(n), and why does the naive rolling-array approach fail here?
  • What's the relationship between this problem and Stone Game II or III?

Worth answering that even-length question directly, since it's a great one to think through: with an even number of elements, Player 1 can commit to a strategy of taking only even-indexed piles (or only odd-indexed ones, whichever total is larger) — and no matter how Player 2 plays, that parity of positions stays available to Player 1 throughout the game. That guarantees Player 1 at least a tie, and often more once the DP factors in actual optimal play beyond that baseline strategy.

Key Takeaways

The real lesson in Predict the Winner isn't the code — it's the reduction. Whenever you see a two-player, zero-sum, alternating-turn game with additive payoffs, ask whether you actually need to track both players' state, or whether a single relative value captures everything that matters. That one shift turns a two-dimensional state space into something with half the moving parts, and it's a pattern that generalizes directly to a whole family of harder game-theory DP problems.
